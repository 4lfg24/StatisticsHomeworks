Addressing the core homework requirements: comparing encryption/decryption procedures, key spaces, the role of keys, and statistical resistance to cryptanalysis.

Shannon's Confusion & Diffusion

Claude Shannon established that secure ciphers must maximize confusion (making the relationship between the key and the ciphertext as complex and non-linear as possible) and diffusion (dissipating plaintext statistical redundancy across the entire ciphertext). The Caesar cipher features zero diffusion and linear confusion: each ciphertext character depends on exactly one plaintext letter and a scalar addition. In contrast, elliptic-curve scalar multiplication Q = kG is an algebraic one-way function exhibiting pseudo-random coordinate dispersion across finite field coordinates.

Semantic Security (IND-CPA)

A cipher is indistinguishable under chosen-plaintext attack (IND-CPA) if an adversary who submits two equal-length messages M₀ and M₁ cannot determine with probability significantly greater than 1/2 which one was encrypted. Because Caesar is deterministic, an adversary immediately distinguishes whether "A" encrypted to "D" or "Z". In ECC schemes, the ephemeral scalar r ensures that encrypting the word "HELLO" twice yields two completely uncorrelated ciphertexts, achieving semantic security.

Bridge to Task 2 & Task 3

The profound differences exposed here establish the mathematical foundation for the remaining tasks: In Task 2, we exploit the rigid frequency-preservation property of Caesar to build an automated statistical cryptanalysis engine that cracks the shift key using the Chi-squared test and letter distributions. In Task 3, we show that attacking the ECC scheme cannot be done statistically; instead, it requires solving the Discrete Logarithm Problem, which is trivial only when the key space is artificially restricted as in our toy demonstration.
