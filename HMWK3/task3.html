Formal Academic Discussion

Cryptanalytic Comparison & Architectural Weaknesses

Addressing the core homework requirements: comparing the ECC attack with Caesar cryptanalysis, explaining the impossibility of attacks against 256-bit ECC, and analyzing design weaknesses of the toy scheme.

1. ECC Attack vs Caesar Statistical Attack

The two cryptanalytic approaches operate on completely separate theoretical planes:

• Caesar Cryptanalysis (Task 2) is an information-theoretic attack exploiting plaintext redundancy. It does not invert a mathematical one-way function; it relies on the fact that natural language letters possess a highly non-uniform probability distribution (H(X) < log₂ 26). If the plaintext were uniformly random bytes or a One-Time Pad, statistical cryptanalysis would fail completely.

• ECC Cryptanalysis (Task 3) is an algebraic computational attack solving the Discrete Logarithm Problem. It exploits computational under-dimensioning (an artificially small key space k ∈ [1, 18]). It requires zero knowledge of natural language statistics, works on completely random binary payloads, and attacks the algebraic group homomorphism directly.

2. Why the Attack Fails on Properly Configured ECC

Against a standardized 256-bit elliptic curve like secp256k1, the private key space is:
n = 1.15792089... × 10⁷⁷ ≈ 2²⁵⁶
• Brute-force enumeration requires 2²⁵⁶ point additions.
• The Square-Root Barrier: The fastest known classical generic algorithms (BSGS, Pollard's rho) require O(√n) ≈ 2¹²⁸ ≈ 3.4 × 10³⁸ operations.

Even if we commandeered the entire global Bitcoin mining network (~600 Exahashes/second = 6 × 10²⁰ SHA256/s, equivalent to billions of specialized ASICs), solving one 256-bit discrete logarithm would require:
(3.4 × 10³⁸) / (6 × 10²⁰) ≈ 5.67 × 10¹⁷ seconds ≈ 18 BILLION YEARS
This is substantially longer than the entire age of the universe (~13.8 billion years) and physically impossible by Bremermann's and Landauer's thermodynamic limits of computation.

3. Weaknesses of Restricted Keys vs Toy Scheme Design

Weaknesses from Restricted Key Space:
• Precomputation / Rainbow Tables: Adversaries can precompute all kG pairs offline in 0.001 ms, creating an instant lookup table that cracks future sessions in $O(1)$ memory lookups.

Weaknesses Arising from the Design of the Toy Scheme Itself:
• Composite Subgroup Order (Pohlig-Hellman Attack): Over 𝔽₁₇, the curve order is n = 18 = 2 × 3². By the Pohlig-Hellman algorithm, the discrete log can be solved independently in subgroups of order 2 and 9 and recombined via the Chinese Remainder Theorem. Total work is reduced from 18 to 2 + 3 = 5 operations! Standard curves explicitly demand a large prime order n to defeat Pohlig-Hellman.
• Small Subgroup Confinement: An attacker sending points not on the main generator can leak private key residues mod small factors.
• Ciphertext Malleability: Textbook ElGamal lacks authenticated encryption (AEAD). An attacker can alter C₂' = C₂ + Δ to systematically alter decrypted plaintext.
